# Editor Pod Design

Editors leave content operations for a consistent reason: the work becomes
mechanical. Someone hired to make things spends a year making versions of
things, and the craft they joined for atrophies. Volume operations that
retain talent are the ones that deliberately protect the making.

Structure is the first lever, and most teams pick the wrong axis.

## Choosing the axis

| Axis | Strength | Failure mode |
|---|---|---|
| By channel | Deep platform fluency | Silos; insight doesn't cross |
| By format | Craft specialisation | Bottlenecks when demand shifts |
| By concept or campaign | Ownership end to end | Uneven load, duplicated learning |
| Hybrid pods | Balanced | Needs real clarity or it's just matrix |

For a small team carrying a broad portfolio, pods organised around *lanes*
usually beat pods organised around channels: a fast-lane pod optimised for
throughput and iteration, a high-stakes pod optimised for craft and finish,
with deliberate rotation between them.

Rotation is the point. Without it the fast pod burns out and the
high-stakes pod becomes a protected class, which is corrosive in both
directions.

## A great week for an editor

Define it explicitly, because if you don't, the answer defaults to "cleared
the queue". A defensible shape:

- One thing made that they'd show someone — not a version, a thing
- Volume work delivered without heroics
- One piece of specific craft feedback received, from a person, on the work
- One piece of information about what happened to their work in market
- Time that was not fragmented across five briefs

The fourth is the one that gets dropped and the one editors miss most.
Editors who never learn how their work performed are being asked to develop
taste without evidence.

## Craft development

Three mechanisms, in rising order of cost and value:

1. **Reference practice** — regular short sessions dissecting work, including
   competitors'. Cheap, and it builds shared vocabulary that makes every
   later feedback conversation faster.
2. **Stretch assignment with a safety net** — an editor takes a piece one
   level above their current lane, with a named partner. This is how people
   actually level up; courses are not.
3. **Ownership of a surface** — an editor owns a channel, a format, or a
   distinctive asset end to end, including its performance. Ownership
   develops judgement in a way that assignments cannot.

## Levels and feedback

Write a craft ladder with observable behaviours rather than years of
experience — what someone can do unsupervised, what they can brief others to
do, what they can define standards for. Ambiguous levels are the most common
source of retention loss among strong mid-level editors, because they cannot
see the path and assume there isn't one.

Separate the two feedback types explicitly. **Craft feedback** is frequent,
specific, on the work, and safe. **Performance feedback** is scheduled, about
the pattern, and never a surprise. Collapsing them makes every review of a
cut feel like a referendum on the person, and editors stop taking risks.

## Underperformance

Diagnose before acting. Genuinely underperforming editors are rarer than
underperforming *conditions*: unclear briefs, an inaccessible approver,
missing references, or a lane mismatch. Check those first — most apparent
performance problems in high-volume operations are systems problems wearing a
person's name.

When it is genuinely the person, be specific, be early, and be explicit about
what changes and by when. Vague dissatisfaction is more damaging than a
direct conversation, and everyone else on the team can already see it.

## First hires

Hire in this order for a volume operation: a **senior editor who sets the
craft bar** by example and can review others' work; a **producer or content
operations lead** who owns intake, scheduling and metadata, because that role
buys back more capacity than another editor does; then a **versatile
mid-level editor** who can rotate across lanes. Specialists come after the
system holds.
