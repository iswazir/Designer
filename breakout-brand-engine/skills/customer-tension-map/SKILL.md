# Customer Tension Map

Breakout brands rarely win on a better feature. They win by saying out loud
the thing the category has agreed to keep quiet. Oatly said dairy marketing
was aimed at cows, not humans. Liquid Death said bottled water was boring
and sold it like a hardcore record. Poppi said soda was a guilty pleasure
and refused the guilt.

The job of this skill is to find that unsaid thing before you write a word
of copy, because a well-executed ad on a tension nobody feels is still a
dead ad.

## Find the tension

Work through four layers. The tension you want is usually in layer three or
four — the first two are what customers say in surveys, which is where most
briefs stop.

1. **Stated problem** — what they say they want. ("I want more energy.")
2. **Functional friction** — what actually annoys them. (Six bottles on the
   counter, none of them finished.)
3. **Social tension** — what it costs them in front of other people. (Taking
   supplements reads as either vanity or hypochondria, depending on the room.)
4. **Identity tension** — the contradiction they live with. (They want to be
   the kind of person who has their health handled, and the routine itself
   is evidence they don't.)

Write the tension as a sentence the customer would recognise but has never
said aloud. If it sounds like a benefit statement, go deeper.

## Pressure-test it

A usable tension survives these four questions:

- **Is it true for a specific person?** Name them. Not "health-conscious
  35–54" — a 41-year-old who has restarted a routine four times.
- **Does the category actively avoid it?** If competitors say it in their
  ads, it is not a tension, it is a convention.
- **Does the product resolve it honestly?** A tension your product cannot
  answer becomes a promise you cannot keep, and in regulated categories that
  is a claims problem, not just a creative one.
- **Would saying it out loud make someone uncomfortable?** Mild discomfort is
  the signal. Comfort means you found a platitude.

## Turn it into creative territory

For each tension, produce three assets:

| Output | What it is |
|---|---|
| Tension line | One sentence, customer's voice, never used in the ad verbatim |
| Provocation | The line that *is* used — the tension said out loud, with brand attitude |
| Proof obligation | What the brand must show to earn the provocation |

The proof obligation is the part teams skip, and it's the part that keeps a
provocative brand from becoming a liability. If the provocation is "most
multivitamins are guesswork", the proof obligation is real substantiation on
what makes yours not guesswork — which is where `health-claim-gate` picks up.

## Worked shape

Tension: *"I've spent years assembling a routine and I still can't tell
anyone what it's actually doing for me."*

Provocation: the brand names the incoherence of the stack directly, rather
than adding one more bottle to it.

Proof obligation: ingredient rationale, dose transparency, and trial evidence
strong enough that "coherent" is a fact and not a mood.

## Common failure

Teams find a tension, love it, and then let it drift into generic aspiration
over successive rounds because the sharp version is scarier to approve. Keep
the original sentence at the top of every brief. When creative stops mapping
to it, you have either found a new tension or lost the plot — decide which
on purpose.
