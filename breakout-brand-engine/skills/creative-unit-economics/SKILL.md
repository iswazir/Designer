# Creative Unit Economics

Most content leaders describe their operation in volume and their results in
platform ROAS, and are then unable to answer the question a CFO actually
asks: what does a winner cost, and would one more editor pay for itself?

Being able to answer that changes the conversation from defending a cost
centre to sizing an investment. It is also the discipline that stops volume
targets from becoming their own justification.

## The four numbers

**1. Cost per shipped asset.** Fully loaded — salaries, freelancers, agency
fees, tooling, licensing, and the production amortised across its
derivatives. Useful mainly as a trend and a make-versus-buy input; on its own
it rewards cheap volume, so never manage to it alone.

**2. Cost per winning asset.** Total creative cost divided by the number of
assets that graduate to scale. This is the number that matters, and it
reframes the operation: you are not manufacturing assets, you are buying
winners, and everything else is the cost of finding them.

**3. Win rate by lane and source.** What share of concepts graduate, split by
in-house, freelance, agency, creator, and AI-assisted. Sources differ sharply
here, and the differences rarely match their cost ranking.

**4. Incremental contribution.** What conversion volume the creative actually
caused, from geo tests, holdouts, or matched-market experiments — not
platform-reported attribution, which credits creative for conversions that
would have happened anyway.

## Why incrementality matters to a creative team

Platform attribution rewards creative that finds people already close to
buying. Optimise on it long enough and the account fills with efficient
retargeting-style work while genuinely new demand quietly stops arriving.
Growth then stalls at a healthy-looking ROAS, which is the most confusing
failure mode in performance marketing because every dashboard says you're
winning.

You do not need continuous incrementality measurement — a periodic read on
major graduations is enough to keep the ladder honest.

## The headcount case

Build it as a chain, not an assertion: an editor produces *n* concepts per
month at win rate *w*, giving *n×w* winners, each contributing measured
incremental volume *v*. Compare *n×w×v* against fully loaded cost.

State the assumptions explicitly and show the break-even win rate. Executives
trust a model whose failure conditions are visible far more than a
confident number, and the break-even framing turns a budget argument into a
shared risk assessment.

## Make-versus-buy

Compare on cost per *winning* asset, not per asset, and hold two things
constant that teams usually forget: brief quality, and who absorbs rework.
Cheap external capacity with a high rework rate and a low win rate is
frequently more expensive than in-house once both are counted — and the
in-house version also compounds craft, which does not appear in either number.
See `editor-pod-design`.
