# Distributed Operating Rhythm

Distance costs decisions, not hours. A team split across a wide time gap can
work the same total hours as a co-located one and still move at half the
speed, because every unresolved question costs a full day. The design problem
is minimising the number of things that must wait for overlap.

## Start from the overlap window

Map the genuine overlap — the hours both sides are awake and functional, not
technically online. For a US-to-Asia split this is typically two to three
hours, at the start of one day and the end of the other.

That window is the scarcest resource in the operating model. Protect it for
the only two things that genuinely need synchrony:

1. **Decisions requiring judgement from both sides**
2. **Conversations where tone matters** — feedback, disagreement, anything
   with interpersonal weight

Everything else — status, handoffs, briefs, approvals with clear criteria —
should be asynchronous by design. A status meeting inside the overlap window
is an expensive way to read a document aloud.

## Structure the week

A workable shape for two hubs:

- **Start of week, in overlap** — priorities, trade-offs, what comes out to
  let something in. Short, decisions only, agenda published beforehand.
- **Mid-week, async** — written performance read circulated by one hub with
  a required written response window before the next overlap.
- **End of week, in overlap** — creative review of precedent-setting work,
  live, because craft feedback survives poorly in writing.
- **Continuous, async** — a decision log that records what was decided, by
  whom, and why. This is the artefact that lets the sleeping hub act on
  waking rather than wait for a recap.

## Push authority toward the work

The structural fix for time-zone lag is not better meetings, it is fewer
decisions needing to cross the gap. Each hub needs real decision rights
inside defined bounds — spend thresholds, creative lanes, kill decisions —
so the common case resolves locally and only genuine exceptions travel.

Write those bounds down. Undocumented authority defaults to asking, and
asking costs a day.

## Rotate the burden

If one hub always takes the uncomfortable hour, that hub carries a hidden tax
that shows up eventually as attrition. Alternate which side stretches, or
split the difference so both are mildly inconvenienced. Being seen to share
the cost matters more than the hours themselves.

## Written-first as a discipline

Distributed teams that move fast are almost always writing-heavy: briefs,
decision logs, performance reads, and feedback that stands alone without a
meeting. The investment is real and it is the highest-leverage habit
available — every written artefact converts a synchronous dependency into an
asynchronous one.

## Signals it isn't working

Decisions repeatedly deferred to "next overlap", the same question asked in
both directions, work restarted after a handoff, and one hub consistently
learning about decisions rather than making them. That last one is a
delegation problem, not a scheduling one.
