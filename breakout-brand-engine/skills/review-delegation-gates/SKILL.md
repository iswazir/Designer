# Review Delegation Gates

A leader who reviews everything caps the operation at their own throughput.
A leader who reviews nothing discovers problems in market. The resolution is
not a middle amount of reviewing — it is reviewing *different things*: the
decisions that set precedent, and samples that reveal drift.

## Sort by reversibility and precedent

Two questions decide who reviews an asset:

- **How expensive is being wrong?** A paid-social variant can be killed in
  minutes. A billboard cannot.
- **Does this set a precedent?** The first asset in a new format, the first
  use of a new claim, the first execution of a new code — these teach the
  team what "right" looks like and get copied for months.

This yields a clean rule: **personally review the first, the irreversible,
and the exceptional. Delegate the repeated.** The first asset of a new
concept is worth an hour of your attention; the fortieth variant of it is
worth none.

## What must never be delegated

A small, explicit list — and its shortness is what makes it credible:

- Any new health or benefit claim, or a new use of an existing one
- Anything featuring ambassador or talent likeness beyond approved cuts
- Anything irreversible at scale: TV, OOH, PR
- The first execution in a new format, code, or voice register
- Anything a reviewer has escalated, for any reason or none

That final item matters more than it looks. An escalation route with no
threshold — anyone may escalate anything without justifying it — is what
makes the rest of the delegation safe, because it removes the judgement call
about whether something is "worth" your time.

## Guardrails that make delegation work

Delegation fails when people are given authority without the means to
exercise it well. Four supports:

1. **Binary checks before taste checks.** Codes present, claim language
   verbatim from the approved set, disclosure present, spec correct. Binary
   checks are delegable and automatable; taste checks are neither.
2. **A written quality bar with examples.** Approved and rejected pairs, with
   the reason. Standards travel through examples, not adjectives.
3. **Named reviewers with real authority.** A reviewer who cannot approve is
   an extra step, not a gate.
4. **A published SLA.** A gate with no turnaround commitment becomes an
   unbounded queue, and unbounded queues are how people learn to route around
   the process.

## Sampling instead of gating

For fast-lane work, replace inspection with sampling. Pull a random sample
weekly, review it properly, and publish what you found — including what was
good. Sampling catches drift, teaches the standard publicly, and costs a
fraction of gating.

Rising defect rates in the sample are the signal to tighten; sustained clean
samples are permission to loosen. Make those adjustments visible so the team
sees the system responding to evidence rather than to mood.

## Testing whether you are still the bottleneck

Track how long work waits on you specifically, and what share of your reviews
result in a change. A review that changes nothing 95% of the time is
ceremony — delegate it and spend the attention on the precedent-setting work
that actually shapes the operation.
