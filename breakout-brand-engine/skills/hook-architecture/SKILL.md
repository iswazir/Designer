# Hook Architecture

The first second decides whether the other twenty-nine exist. But a hook is
not a trick bolted onto a good ad — it is a promise, and the body of the ad
either pays it or the viewer feels conned and the brand pays instead.

Treat hooks as a structured space you can search systematically, not as
inspiration you wait for. That is what lets a team generate forty genuinely
different openings instead of forty rewrites of one.

## Seven hook families

Each opens a different loop in the viewer's head. Rotating families is how
you avoid fatigue; rotating words within one family is how you cause it.

1. **Contradiction** — states something that shouldn't be true. *"The
   healthiest thing I did this year was quit four supplements."*
2. **In-progress** — drops the viewer mid-action, no setup. Works because the
   brain resists leaving an unfinished scene.
3. **Named audience** — calls the segment out loud. Sacrifices reach for
   precision; strong on cold traffic that's been mis-served.
4. **Demonstration** — shows the mechanism physically. Powder dissolving,
   labels compared side by side. Highest credibility per second.
5. **Confession** — admits a real cost or failure. Earns trust fast; only
   works if the admission is genuine.
6. **Question with stakes** — not "want more energy?" but a question whose
   wrong answer costs something.
7. **Authority collision** — two credible sources disagreeing. Powerful and
   the most dangerous in regulated categories; verify with `health-claim-gate`.

## The payoff contract

Write the hook and the payoff line at the same time, in the same sitting. If
you cannot write the payoff, the hook is a lie you haven't noticed yet.

The test: could a viewer who watched only the first second and the last
second tell they belong to the same ad? If not, the hook is arbitrage on
attention and it will show up later as poor hold rate, weak conversion, and
comment sections arguing with you.

## Generate a slate

For a testing round, produce a grid: entry points from
`category-entry-points` down the side, hook families across the top. Fill
only the cells that make sense — a demonstration hook on a 6am kitchen
moment works; an authority collision usually doesn't.

Twenty filled cells give twenty structurally distinct tests. That is a clean
read, because when one wins you learn which *family* works for that moment,
not merely which sentence did.

## Craft notes

- **Cut the runway.** Most hooks start half a second late. Delete the first
  clause and check whether anything was lost.
- **Specificity beats intensity.** "Ninety active ingredients" outperforms
  "incredibly comprehensive" because a number is checkable and adjectives are
  free.
- **Say one thing.** A hook carrying two ideas carries none.
- **Read it aloud.** Copy that a person cannot say naturally will be
  delivered badly by every creator you send it to.

## What not to do

Curiosity gaps that resolve into nothing, fake-native openings that imitate
platform UI, and borrowed urgency ("doctors are furious") all buy a spike and
sell the brand's credibility to fund it. In a health category they also
attract exactly the regulatory attention you least want.
