# Claim to Copy

The standard failure in science-led brands is a false choice: either copy
that moves people and worries counsel, or copy that clears review and moves
nobody. Both are avoidable. Flat compliant copy is usually not a legal
constraint — it is a craft failure wearing a legal excuse.

The move is to be *more* specific, not vaguer. Vagueness feels safer and
often isn't: regulators read the net impression, and a vague line that
implies a disease benefit is worse than a precise line that doesn't.

## The translation ladder

Work down from the evidence, never up from the desired headline.

1. **What was measured** — the actual endpoint, population, duration, dose.
2. **What that supports** — the claim the evidence carries, stated dryly.
3. **What it means to a person** — the lived consequence, still literal.
4. **How it feels** — the emotional register, carrying no new assertion.

Step four is where the craft lives, and the rule is simple: rungs three and
four may change the *language*, never the *scope*. If step four is broader
than step two, you have written a claim your evidence cannot fund.

## Techniques that keep power without inflating scope

- **Trade adjectives for numbers.** Specific quantities feel more confident
  than superlatives and are defensible.
- **Describe the mechanism, not the outcome.** How something works is
  interesting, often permissible, and rarely a disease claim.
- **Let structure carry emotion.** Rhythm, sentence length, and contrast
  create feeling without adding assertions.
- **Use the honest limit as a credibility move.** Naming what a product does
  *not* do reads as confidence. Most competitors won't do it.
- **Put the human in the sentence, not in the claim.** A person in a real
  moment can carry warmth the claim itself must not.

## Register by rung

Credibility copy has a specific voice: calm, concrete, unhurried. Brand
atmosphere applied to an evidence question reads as evasion — see
`objection-ladder` for why that rung fails. The most trustworthy line in an
ad is usually its plainest.

## Danger patterns

These convert well and are exactly why they persist:

- Before-and-after framing that implies treatment of a condition
- Testimonials describing recovery, even unprompted and genuine
- Symptom lists that collectively describe a diagnosable disease
- Stacking permissible statements so the *combination* implies a disease claim
- Borrowed authority — a coat, a stethoscope, a clinical set — that implies
  medical endorsement the brand doesn't have

The last three are the ones teams miss, because each element passes review
alone. Review the finished asset as a whole, not as parts.
